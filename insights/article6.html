The Fake Cohiba Test: How Counterfeits Are Priced, Spotted, and Sold

Cohiba is the most counterfeited cigar brand on earth. The fakes are priced just below genuine, and the tells are printed in plain sight — on the band, the box, and the seal.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The box was perfect. The band was perfect. The price was $140 for a box of ten Behikes that retails for more than $1,500, and the seller had a story about a cousin in Havana and a diplomatic pouch. The buyer, a collector in Miami who had been smoking Cuban cigars for twenty years, knew all of this and bought the box anyway. He opened it at home, smoked one, and knew within three draws. The draw was wrong, the ash was wrong, the taste was wrong. The box was a counterfeit, and the counterfeit was good enough that the only thing that gave it away was the smoking of it — which is to say, the only test that matters is the one you cannot perform before you pay.

Cohiba is the most counterfeited cigar brand on earth. It is counterfeited because it is the most famous, the most expensive, and the most desired, and because the gap between the price of a genuine Cohiba and the price of a fake is large enough to make the counterfeit business worth running. The fakes are not sold at a tenth of the price. They are sold at a price just low enough to be plausible and just high enough to be profitable. The counterfeit market is a pricing market before it is a forgery market, and the buyer who understands the price is the buyer who does not get caught.

Why Cohiba, and why the fakes are priced the way they are

Cohiba was created in 1966 at the El Laguito factory in Havana, where it was rolled for Fidel Castro and for diplomatic gifts. It was not sold to the public until 1982. The Behike, the most counterfeited cigar in the Cohiba line, was introduced in 2006 as a limited release and became its own line in 2010. The brand's history is the history of scarcity: a cigar that was not for sale for sixteen years, and a line that was a limited edition before it was a product. Scarcity creates a price, and a price creates a counterfeit.

The counterfeit price is set by the genuine price, and the genuine price is high. A box of Behike 52, the smallest of the line, trades at roughly $200 a cigar. A box of the 1492, the largest, sold for $31,000 at auction in 2018. A fake Behike sold on a Havana street goes for $10. A fake sold online, in a convincing box with a convincing band, goes for $60 to $100 a cigar — a price that is low enough to feel like a deal and high enough to feel like a genuine discount. The counterfeiter's pricing is a piece of applied psychology: the price must be low enough to attract the buyer and high enough not to alarm him. The fakes that are priced at a tenth of retail are not the dangerous ones. The dangerous ones are priced at two-thirds.

The band: the first test, and the easiest to fail

The band is where most fakes fail, and it is where the genuine article is most distinctive. A real Cohiba band has a holographic image of the head of the Taíno Indian chief Hatuey, with a smaller head visible inside it, and the hologram shifts as the band is turned. Below the head are nine rows of white squares. Around the border, the words COHIBA and CUBA are microprinted in a pattern that is visible under magnification and blurred on a photocopy. The band also carries ultraviolet-reactive fibres that are invisible in normal light and visible under a UV lamp. The white of the band is a clean, bright white; the gold is a flat, matte gold, not a shiny one.

The triple cap is the second test. Every hand-rolled Cuban cigar is finished with three layers of tobacco leaf at the head, applied in a spiral. The caps are neat, even and tight. A fake, rolled quickly and by hand outside a factory, often has a cap that is uneven, or a single cap, or a cap that unravels when the cigar is cut. The cap is a small detail and it is a reliable one, because it is the detail the counterfeiter is least likely to get right: it takes time, and time is what the counterfeiter does not have.

The third test is the box. A genuine box of Cohiba carries a hot-iron stamp reading Habanos S.A., a stamp reading HECHO EN CUBA, and a stamp reading Totalmente a mano — totally by hand. The box carries a factory code of three letters, and the code matters: Cohiba is made at El Laguito, whose code is EML. A box of Cohiba with any other factory code is a fake, whatever else it says. The box also carries a date stamp and a four-digit batch code, which gives the year and the week of production.

The seal and the website: the only test that settles it

Since 1999, every box of Cuban cigars has carried a warranty seal, and since 2009 the seal has included a hologram. The seal carries a barcode, and the barcode can be checked on the Habanos verification website, verificacion.habanos.com. The check is free, it takes a minute, and it is the only test that settles the question. A box that passes the band test, the cap test and the box test can still be a fake. A box whose barcode does not verify is a fake, whatever it looks like. The verification site is the reason the counterfeiters have moved from forging boxes to forging barcodes, and the reason the serious buyer checks the code before paying rather than after.

The verification step is also the reason the most reliable advice in the counterfeit literature is the simplest: buy from an authorised dealer. Habanos S.A. maintains a network of authorised retailers, and the cigars sold through it are genuine by definition. The discount available from an unauthorised seller is the price of the risk, and the risk is that the cigars are not what they claim to be. The collector who buys a box of Behikes at a third of the retail price from a seller he found on a messaging app has not found a bargain. He has found the counterfeit market's pricing strategy working as intended.

The counterargument: some fakes are real Cuban tobacco

There is a complication, and it is worth stating. Not every counterfeit Cohiba is made from cheap tobacco. Some are rolled in Cuba, by rollers who learned the trade in the state factories, using genuine Cuban tobacco that left the factory through informal channels. These cigars smoke well. They are not Cohibas — they were not made in the factory, they were not aged in the factory's conditions, and they do not carry the factory's quality control — but they are not the floor sweepings that the word counterfeit suggests. The distinction matters because it explains why the counterfeit market survives its own reputation: some buyers know the cigars are fake, and buy them anyway, because they smoke well enough for the price. The counterfeit market is not only a fraud market. It is also a grey market, and the grey market's customers are not all victims.

That does not make the fakes a good buy. A cigar that is not a Cohiba is priced as a Cohiba, and the buyer is paying for a name that is not on the cigar. The grey-market cigar is a fair price for a good cigar and a bad price for a Cohiba. The test is the same either way: the band, the cap, the box, the code, and the verification website. A cigar that fails the tests is not a Cohiba, whatever it tastes like.
