The Vintage Cigar Market: What a 20-Year-Old Box Is Worth, and Why

A box of Cohiba Behike from 2010 trades for about $200 a cigar. A 40th Anniversary humidor sold for $156,000. Age adds value to a cigar the way it does to nothing else in the humidor — when the box, the code, and the provenance all agree.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The lot came up at 9:40 in the evening, and the room went quiet in the way auction rooms go quiet when the number is about to get large. It was a humidor, not a box of cigars: the Cohiba Behike 40th Anniversary humidor, a numbered piece made for the fortieth anniversary of the Cohiba brand. The hammer fell at $156,000. In the same sale, a Cohiba 30th Anniversary humidor went for $52,000, and a 30th Anniversary jar for $7,200. The cigars inside were, by any ordinary measure, worth a fraction of those prices. The prices were not for the cigars. They were for the age, the number, and the box — and for the fact that there would never be another one like them.

The vintage cigar market is the smallest and strangest corner of the luxury market. It is a market in a product that is meant to be consumed, that deteriorates if it is stored badly, that improves if it is stored well, and that has a peak after which it declines. A bottle of wine can be kept for a century. A cigar cannot. The window is twenty to thirty years, and the market prices the window.

What age does to a cigar, and what it does to the price

A cigar improves with age for the first decade or two, as the tobacco mellows and the flavours integrate, and then it declines, as the oils that carry the flavour evaporate. The market knows this, and the prices reflect it. A box of Cohiba Behike 52 from the 2010 production year traded at about $200 a cigar in 2018. A box of the 1492, the largest Behike, sold for $31,000 at auction in 2018 — $637 a cigar. A box of Cohiba 1966 Edición Limitada from 2011, which retailed at $400 to $500, now sells at auction for $800 to $1,200, according to CigarDesk, a collector's guide. The pattern is consistent: the limited editions and the anniversary releases appreciate. The regular production cigars, aged the same way, do not.

The most extreme prices are for the objects that are not really cigars at all. Six loose Dunhill Estupendo cigars — a brand that ceased production decades ago — sold for about $1,077 each at auction in 2012. The price was for the rarity and the provenance, not for the smoking experience. A cigar that old is a museum piece that happens to be smokeable. The buyer is not buying a smoke. He is buying a story, and the story is priced by the auction room.

CigarDesk, the collector's guide, claims that the top lots in the vintage market return 8 to 15 percent a year, and that a box held for ten years or more can trade at three to five times its retail price. The claim should be read as a guide's claim, not as a market index: there is no index for vintage cigars, no published transaction database, and no way to verify the average. What is verifiable is the auction record, and the auction record shows that the winners are a narrow set — Cohiba Edición Limitadas, Behikes, anniversary humidors, pre-embargo Dunhills — and that the median aged box is not among them.

The auction record is the only public price data the market has. Cigar Aficionado publishes the results of the major sales, lot by lot, and the figures in this article come from its reporting. There is no index, no published transaction database, and no way to check the average return across the market. A buyer who wants to know what a box is worth must read the auction reports, lot by lot, and compare the box in front of him with the boxes that have actually sold. The market is transparent at the top and opaque everywhere else, and the opacity is part of the price.

The three conditions: the box, the code, and the provenance

A vintage cigar is worth what it is worth only if three conditions are met. The first is the box: the original box, in good condition, with the seals intact. A box that has been opened and resealed, or a box with a damaged seal, is worth less, because the buyer cannot be sure the cigars inside are the cigars that were put there. The second is the code: the factory code and the date stamp on the bottom of the box, which establish where and when the cigars were made. A box without a legible code is a box without a story, and a box without a story is priced as a box of old cigars.

The third condition is provenance, and it is the one that costs the most. The auction houses that handle vintage cigars — C.Gars Ltd in London, run by Mitchell Orchant, and the specialist sales at the major houses — take a commission of 15 percent plus VAT from the seller, and they take it because they authenticate the lot. The authentication is the product. A box bought from a private seller, without an auction house's authentication, trades at a discount, because the buyer is taking the risk that the box is not what it claims to be. The counterfeit problem that affects new Cohibas affects vintage Cohibas more, because the price is higher and the evidence is older.

Storage is the fourth condition, and it is the one the buyer cannot verify from a photograph. A cigar must be kept at 65 to 70 degrees Fahrenheit and 65 to 68 percent relative humidity, continuously, for the whole of its life. A box that spent five years in a hot attic is worth nothing, whatever its code says. The auction houses ask for the storage history, and the sellers who can document it — a cabinet with a data logger, a humidor with a service record — get the premium. The sellers who cannot, do not.

The counterargument: most aged cigars lose money

The counterargument to the vintage cigar market is the market itself. Most aged cigars do not appreciate. A box of regular production cigars, bought at retail and stored well for ten years, is worth roughly what it cost, or less, once the cost of storage and the seller's commission are counted. The 15 percent commission, plus VAT, takes a sixth of the sale price before the seller sees a penny. The electricity and the humidity control cost $70 a year. The insurance, if the collection is insured, costs more. A box that doubles in price over ten years has returned about 7 percent a year before costs, and about 4 percent after them — a return that a savings account would have matched, without the risk that the cigars dry out.

And unlike wine, a cigar's peak is a window, not a plateau. A wine can be kept past its peak and still be a good wine. A cigar kept past its peak is a dry cigar, and a dry cigar is worth nothing. The collector who holds a box for thirty years has not made it more valuable. He has held it past the point where it was valuable, and the market will price it accordingly. The vintage cigar market rewards the collector who sells at the peak, and it punishes the collector who holds for the story.

The honest version of the vintage cigar market is this: it is a market for a narrow set of cigars, bought well, stored perfectly, and sold at the right moment, through an auction house that authenticates them. The returns on those cigars are real, and they are large. The returns on the rest of the market are not. The buyer who understands the difference is the buyer who makes money. The buyer who does not is the buyer who owns a humidor full of old cigars and a story about what they are worth.
