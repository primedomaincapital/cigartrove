The Room Where the List Matters More Than the Shelf

The shop that sells everything to everyone competes on price and loses. The club that knows its members competes on judgment, and judgment is much harder to copy.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · October 29, 2026 · 5 min read

The door closed behind him with a weight that had nothing to do with its hinges. It was after nine on a Thursday in a brick building off a main street that most drivers passed without a second look, and inside, the air was cedar and leather and low conversation. A man behind the counter did not ask what the visitor wanted. He asked what he had smoked last week, what he had thought of it, and what he planned to drink with the next one. Only then did he open the humidor.

That small exchange is the entire business model of the modern cigar club, and it explains why a curated name can be worth more than a large inventory. The shop that sells everything to everyone competes on price and loses. The club that knows its members competes on judgment, and judgment is much harder to copy.

The numbers behind the trade support the point. The United States imported 430 million premium, handmade cigars in 2024, up 0.9 percent from 426.3 million in 2023, according to data from the Cigar Association of America reported by Cigar Aficionado. That was the fourth consecutive year above 400 million, and a 27 percent rise from the 338 million imported in 2019. Imports had peaked higher — 453.9 million in 2021 and 464.5 million in 2022 — so the market is not exploding. It is holding at a historically high plateau. A plateau rewards the operator who keeps customers, not the one who chases a surge.

Supply is concentrated in ways that make curation a real skill rather than a slogan. Nicaragua shipped 253.1 million cigars to the United States in 2024, or 58.8 percent of the total, up 2.7 percent on the year. The Dominican Republic sent 106 million, down 1.8 percent, and Honduras sent 67.4 million. Together, those three countries account for just over 99 percent of handmade cigars exported to the United States. Costa Rica, the only other producer above one million units, shipped 2.5 million after a 44.9 percent jump. A buyer choosing from that narrow funnel needs a guide. A room that offers ten thousand options without guidance is a warehouse. A room that offers forty, each with a reason, is a club.

Grand View Research put the global cigar and cigarillos market at $54.8 billion in 2024, projecting $62.8 billion by 2026 and $102.2 billion by 2033 at a compound annual growth rate of 7.2 percent from 2025 to 2033. Those headline figures mix mass-market products with premium ones, and they should be read with that caution. The same report found that offline sales still accounted for 83.3 percent of distribution in 2024, while the United States took 83.1 percent of North American revenue. The physical room, in other words, is not a legacy channel waiting to be replaced. It is where most of the money still changes hands, and where a subscription or club brand earns the trust that later makes an online sale possible.

The subscription version of the same idea has its own logic. A monthly selection turns a one-time purchase into a relationship with a calendar. The member does not decide among hundreds of sticks; the member decides whether the curator was right last month. That is a lower-friction decision, and it produces a steadier revenue line than walk-in traffic alone. It also produces data the owner can actually use: which profiles members finish, which they trade away, which they ask about again. A shop learns what sells. A club learns what its people like, and the difference shows up in reorders.

There is a counterargument, and it deserves a fair hearing. Curation can curdle into gatekeeping. A room that is too selective, too knowing, or too pleased with itself will repel the newcomer who might have become a member for twenty years. Price is part of the same risk: a curated list that drifts steadily upward teaches members to comparison-shop, and the internet makes that lesson easy to learn. The strongest clubs answer both objections the same way, by keeping an entry point that is honest rather than cheap — a shorter, milder, well-made selection at a fair price, explained without condescension — and by letting members disagree with the curator in public. A club that cannot survive a member saying "this one was not for me" was never a club. It was a lecture.

The economics also cut against romance. Rent, ventilation, insurance, and climate control for a walk-in humidor are fixed costs that do not fall when a month is slow. Staff who can actually advise, rather than merely transact, cost more than staff who cannot. A name that promises curation raises the bar for every hire and every shelf. The owner who buys a curated brand and then stocks it like a convenience store will be found out within a season, because the members will notice before the accounts do.

What the model offers in return is durability. Tobacco trends come and go; the desire for a room with a door, a chair, and a person who remembers what you smoked last week does not. The pandemic-era surge proved that demand can spike, and the plateau since proved that it can also hold. Businesses built for the spike — wide, shallow, and loud — are the ones now discounting. Businesses built for the hold — narrow, deep, and personal — are the ones renewing memberships.

The implication for anyone building in this space is straightforward. Spend less on breadth and more on judgment. Hire the person who asks the second question. Keep the humidor at a steady 68 to 70 percent relative humidity, keep the list short enough to defend, and keep a record of what members actually enjoyed. A trove is not valuable because it is large. It is valuable because someone chose what went into it, and someone else trusts that choice enough to return.
