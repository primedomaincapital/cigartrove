Four Countries, Four Answers

Cuba, Nicaragua, the Dominican Republic, Honduras — each country answers a different question about what a great cigar is.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · October 29, 2026 · 6 min read

The question comes up in every good cigar room, usually an hour in, usually from someone holding a cigar they like and wondering why. "Which country makes the best cigars?" The tobacconist behind the counter has heard it a thousand times. He will not answer it the way the customer wants. He will point to four shelves — Cuba, Nicaragua, the Dominican Republic, Honduras — and say something closer to the truth: each country answers a different question.

Origin is the first thing a smoker asks about and the last thing that settles anything.

Cuba: the benchmark you cannot buy at home

Start with the country that set the standard and then left the American market. Cuban tobacco from Vuelta Abajo, in Pinar del Río province, built the reputation the rest of the trade still measures itself against: earthy, woody, nutty, with a mineral, faintly salty quality smokers call the "Cuban twang," plus dried fruit, honey, and floral notes. Habanos S.A., the company that markets Cuban cigars worldwide, reported record revenue of $827 million in 2024, up 14.7 percent on the previous year, led by China. Europe took about 54 percent of sales.

Those numbers describe scarcity as much as success. Cuba exported roughly 50 million cigars in 2024, against 93.9 million in 2018, according to figures from Tabacuba reported by Al Jazeera. Revenue rose while volume fell — steep price increases, including Cohiba prices that doubled overnight in 2022, and hurricanes Ian (2022) and Rafael (2024) in the western provinces.

For an American buyer, there is a harder fact. A full trade embargo proclaimed by President John F. Kennedy on February 3, 1962, bars Cuban tobacco from the United States, and the Helms-Burton Act of 1996 wrote the embargo into statute so that only Congress can end it. A brief opening for authorized travelers closed on September 24, 2020, when the Treasury Department's Office of Foreign Assets Control eliminated the personal-import exemption. An American may smoke a Cuban cigar abroad. Bringing one home, or buying or selling one in the United States, is prohibited. That is market context, not legal advice. It is also why Cuban cigars carry their mythology in the United States: the forbidden object is always the most discussed one in the room.

Nicaragua: the powerhouse

If Cuba is the benchmark, Nicaragua is the volume, the range, and increasingly the default answer. The United States imported 430 million premium handmade cigars in 2024, up 0.9 percent from 426.3 million in 2023, the fourth straight year above 400 million and 27 percent above the 338 million of 2019, according to Cigar Association of America data. Nicaragua shipped 253.1 million of them — 58.8 percent, up 2.7 percent on the year. The Dominican Republic sent 106 million, down 1.8 percent. Honduras sent 67.4 million, down 3.3 percent. Together the three account for just over 99 percent of handmade cigars exported to the United States, in a non-Cuban premium market worth roughly $2.5 billion at retail.

Nicaragua earns that share with range, not a single note. Estelí, where most factories stand, grows the strongest leaf: black pepper, earth, espresso, dark cocoa. Jalapa, near the Honduran border, grows a sweeter, creamier leaf in red clay, and its wrappers are prized. Condega sits between the two — cedar, nuts, dry earth, light pepper — and Ometepe island leaf, deep and oily, is used sparingly for spice and sweetness. A blender working those valleys can build anything from mild to full-bodied.

The Dominican Republic: the refiner

The Dominican Republic plays a different role: consistency, smoothness, and the long game. Around Santiago, in the Cibao Valley, Dominican tobacco tends toward the creamy, woody, and nutty, gently spicy rather than aggressive, mild to medium in body. The country's industry is the oldest continuous one outside Cuba: La Aurora, founded outside Santiago in 1903 by Eduardo León Jimenes, still uses long-filler leaves from the same fields. Arturo Fuente, whose Hemingway and OpusX lines anchor many a humidor, and Davidoff's Dominican-made Signature line show the same instinct: control, balance, and a smoke that rarely surprises you in a bad way. That steadiness is both the argument and the knock.

Honduras: the bridge

Honduras is the least discussed of the four and the easiest to underrate. Its heart is Danlí and the neighboring Jamastrán Valley, an extension of Nicaragua's Jalapa Valley barely 50 miles from the Jalapa border, whose soils and climate have drawn comparisons to Pinar del Río itself. Honduran leaf is earthy and spicy, often described as "flinty," medium to full in body, built mostly from Cuban-seed Corojo and Criollo, with Connecticut shade from the windy Talanga Valley. Camacho built its name on 100 percent Honduran puros, and Alec Bradley, named for founder Alan Rubin's two sons, makes its Kintsugi at the Raices Cubanas operation in Danlí. Honduras is the bridge between Nicaragua's power and Cuba's earthiness: it often wins the second cigar of the evening.

What origin can and cannot tell you

Here is the counterargument, and it matters more than any ranking. Origin is a tendency, not a verdict. The same Cuban-seed Corojo grown in three countries tastes different in each, which proves that soil and climate matter — and the fact that a great blender can rescue ordinary leaf, while a careless factory can ruin great leaf, proves that hands matter as much as fields. Fermentation, aging, and construction decide whether a cigar draws cleanly and burns evenly; no country owns those skills. Cuba's own recent history — hurricanes, constrained harvests, prices set for scarcity — is a reminder that a famous origin can also be a fragile supply chain.

There is also the irony at the center of the comparison. The modern map was, in large part, drawn by the embargo itself: Cuban seeds, Cuban methods, and Cuban-trained hands carried the craft to the Dominican Republic, Nicaragua, and Honduras, where it grew into industries that now dwarf the island's output. The four origins are not four separate traditions. They are one tradition, transplanted three times, shaped by three different soils.

The practical close

Match the country to the moment. For complexity and occasion, Cuba remains the reference point, wherever it is legally available. For power and range, Nicaragua is the workhorse. For consistency and refinement, the Dominican Republic is the safest shelf. For earthy depth at a fair price, Honduras is the value that regulars discover on their own.

Stock the spectrum, not a flag. A room that carries only one country's answer will bore the regular and mislead the newcomer. A room that carries all four — and a person behind the counter who can explain the differences — gives the customer something no single origin can: a reason to come back and try the next one.
