What a Humidor Actually Costs to Run: Humidity, Electricity, and the Cigars You Lose

A desktop humidor costs $50 to $500. A cabinet runs $500 to $8,800. The purchase price is the cheap part — the running cost is a subscription to humidity, and the loss rate is the tax on getting it wrong.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The humidor was a gift, a handsome wooden box with a glass top, and it cost $85. The cigars inside it cost $400. Six months later, half the cigars were dry and the other half were mouldy, and the owner had learned the lesson that every cigar smoker learns exactly once: the humidor is not the box. The humidor is the system that keeps the box at 65 to 70 percent humidity and 65 to 70 degrees Fahrenheit, every day, for years. The box is the cheap part. The system is the running cost, and the running cost is a subscription to humidity — plus the tax on getting it wrong, which is paid in cigars.

The mistake is a common one, because the humidor market is priced to make it. A desktop humidor costs $50 to $500. An electric cabinet costs $500 to $8,800. The price tags suggest that the choice is between a cheap box and an expensive box. It is not. The choice is between a box that holds humidity and a box that does not, and the difference between the two is not the price of the box. It is the price of the humidification system, the price of the electricity, and the price of the cigars that are lost while the owner learns how the system works.

The humidification subscription

The most common humidification system in a desktop humidor is a Boveda pack, a two-way humidity control packet that releases or absorbs moisture to hold the air at a set relative humidity. A four-pack of the standard size costs $17.99 to $21.99. Boveda's own guidance is one Size 60 pack for every 25 cigars, and the packs need replacing every two to four months in a wooden humidor, or every six to nine months in an airtight container. MyCigarSite, a cigar retail guide, puts the annual cost of keeping a typical 50-count humidor at the right humidity at $50 to $70 a year, including the packs and the distilled water used for seasoning.

That is the cheapest running cost in the hobby, and it is worth stating plainly, because the humidor market would rather sell a cabinet. A sealed plastic container with Boveda packs — the arrangement cigar smokers call a tupperdor — holds humidity as well as a wooden humidor and costs less than $30 to set up, including the packs. The wooden humidor is furniture. The tupperdor is a storage system. The difference in running cost is zero. The difference in price is the furniture.

The cabinet is a different proposition. An electric humidor — a cabinet with a thermoelectric cooling system and an electronic humidifier — is priced in tiers. Candor, a retailer of electric humidors, puts compact models at $150 to $500, mid-range models at $500 to $2,000 for 250 to 700 cigars, and premium models at $2,000 to $8,800 and above. Candor's planning budget for a mid-range cabinet is $700 to $1,500. The cabinet's advantage is capacity and control: it holds hundreds of cigars at a stable temperature and humidity, and it does so without the daily attention a wooden humidor needs. Its disadvantage is that it is a piece of equipment, and equipment has a running cost.

The electricity bill, and the loss rate

The electricity is the smallest line on the bill, and it is the one people ask about most. A 640-count cabinet lists a power consumption of under 0.28 kilowatt-hours a day, which at a typical American residential electricity rate is under six cents a day, or about $20 a year. A smaller cabinet uses less. The electricity cost of running a humidor is, for practical purposes, nothing. The cost that matters is not the electricity. It is the loss rate.

The loss rate is the tax on getting humidity wrong, and it is the largest cost in the system. A cigar stored too dry loses its oils and its flavour, and it cannot be restored. A cigar stored too wet grows mould, and mould spreads. A cigar stored in a humidor that swings between the two — which is what a poorly sealed wooden humidor does in a house with central heating — is damaged twice. CigarDesk, a collector's guide, puts it bluntly: the same box of cigars, stored in a closet instead of a properly maintained humidor, is worth nothing. The cigars are not merely worth less. They are worth nothing, because a cigar that has dried out or grown mould has no buyer.

Do the maths on a modest collection. Fifty cigars at $15 each is $750. Lose a fifth of them to poor storage over two years and the loss is $150 — three times the annual humidification cost. Lose half, which is what the owner of the $85 gift humidor lost, and the loss is $375, or four and a half times the price of the humidor itself. The humidor is not the cost. The cigars are the cost, and the humidor is the insurance.

Seasoning, and the hidden first cost

There is a first cost that the price tag does not show. A wooden humidor must be seasoned before it is used: the interior cedar must be brought up to humidity slowly, over about fourteen days, using seasoning packs or distilled water, so that the wood does not draw moisture out of the cigars. Boveda's seasoning guidance is four 84 percent packs for a 100-count humidor, for fourteen days, with about 100 grams of distilled water. Skip the seasoning, and the humidor will dry the first cigars put into it within days. The seasoning packs cost about $20. The cigars lost to skipping it cost considerably more.

The electric cabinet has its own hidden cost, which is maintenance. The water reservoir must be filled with distilled water, the filter must be changed, and the seals must be checked. None of this is expensive. All of it is the kind of maintenance that is easy to forget for a month, and a month is long enough for the humidity to drift and the cigars to suffer. The humidor that runs itself is a marketing claim. The humidor that runs itself for a month and then needs attention is a fact.

The counterargument: the cheapest humidor that works is nearly free

The counterargument to all of this is that the humidor industry sells furniture and calls it equipment. The cheapest system that holds 65 to 70 percent humidity reliably is a sealed container and Boveda packs, for under $30, plus $50 to $70 a year in replacement packs. It holds humidity better than most wooden humidors, because it is airtight and the wood is not drawing moisture out of the air. It does not look like anything. It does not sit on a desk and impress a guest. It works.

The cabinet's case is capacity and stability at scale, and it is a real case for a collector with hundreds of cigars. For a smoker with fifty, the cabinet is a luxury, and the wooden humidor is a piece of furniture that happens to store cigars. The honest accounting is this: the running cost of keeping cigars properly is $50 to $70 a year in humidity control and about $20 a year in electricity, and the cost of not keeping them properly is the collection itself. Spend the money on the humidity, not on the box.
