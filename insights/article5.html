Tariffs, Whiplash, and the $155 Cohiba: The Cigar Market's Most Expensive Year

In twelve months the premium cigar trade was taxed, threatened with a 100 percent tariff, rescued by the Supreme Court, and taxed again under a different law. The price of a cigar kept rising through all of it.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 9, 2026 · 6 min read

Walk into a good cigar shop this autumn and the conversation at the register is not about the cigar in your hand. It is about the price tag on it, and the price tag is a policy document. As of August 1, 2026, the Premium Cigar Association listed tariff rates of 12.5 percent for Nicaragua and the Dominican Republic and 10 percent for Honduras — the three countries that account for the overwhelming majority of handmade premium cigars sold in the United States. A year earlier those cigars entered duty-free under the Central American Free Trade Agreement. The trade did not change. The border did.

The whiplash came in layers. In October 2025 the administration threatened to raise Nicaragua's tariff from 18 percent to as high as 100 percent, a figure that would have landed on roughly 60 percent of the premium cigar market, since Nicaragua ships about 253 million of the 430 million premium cigars the United States imported in 2024. In February 2026 the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 in Learning Resources v. Trump that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not authorize the president to impose tariffs, voiding the IEEPA-based reciprocal tariff program outright — including the 18 percent Nicaragua rate and the 10 percent baseline on the Dominican Republic and Honduras. The administration answered by announcing a new 10 percent global tariff under Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, which allows up to 15 percent for up to 150 days. Nicaragua's rate would fall from 18 to 10 percent; the Dominican Republic and Honduras would stay at 10 percent under a different legal theory. Nobody in the trade calls that stability. They call it Tuesday.

Underneath the tariff fight sits a second, quieter schedule. The U.S. Trade Representative's Section 301 investigation into Nicaragua produced a 15 percent tariff on select Nicaraguan imports — but cigars are not expected to be caught by it, because qualifying cigars are shielded by CAFTA-DR's rules of origin. The schedule runs 0 percent additional in 2026, 10 percent in 2027, and 15 percent in 2028, on top of an existing 18 percent tariff tied up in litigation. Should both apply, some Nicaraguan goods could face 33 percent by 2028. Cigars are widely expected to remain exempt as long as CAFTA-DR stands. “Widely expected” is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and everyone in Estelí knows it.

The manufacturers' responses split three ways, and the split is the most revealing part of the year. Crowned Heads, working with factories in Nicaragua, the Dominican Republic, and Honduras, placed a nominal surcharge on wholesale invoices rather than raising prices across the board — and its founder, Jon Huber, said plainly that the surcharge “offsets a portion of the tariff charge to us, but does not cover it entirely.” All Saints went the other direction: in August 2025 it reset its go-to-market strategy, standardized four core lines into three formats at $8, $9, and $10, and cut prices by an average of 14 percent, with some cigars down 23 percent, while absorbing the tariff expense itself. Room101's Matt Booth gave the shortest answer of all: “simply put if it cost me more, it cost my customers more. It's simple math nothing more nothing less.” Three companies, one tariff, three different answers about who pays. Huber also refused the move the numbers would suggest — shifting production between countries — saying trade conditions have “no bearing” on which factory makes a blend, because “my loyalty to our trade partners in business is more important to me than the convenience of ‘moving a brand.’”

The prices kept rising anyway, because tariffs were only the newest reason. Cigar Aficionado's 2025 analysis of 620 cigars found Cuba still the most expensive source by a wide margin, averaging $63.51, with nine of 77 Cubans tasted retailing at $100 or more — more than double the 2024 count — and the Cohiba Esplendido at £112.60, or $155.29, the most expensive single cigar rated in the year. Nicaragua's average price of $14.92 was only 21 cents above the prior year, but 14 Nicaraguans retailed at $30 or more, including the Padrón 60th Anniversary Perfecto at $75, which became Cigar of the Year. Imports stayed strong through the noise: 318.6 million premium cigars reached the United States in the first three quarters of 2025, up 4.6 percent, with Nicaragua at 190.4 million, the Dominican Republic at 69.9 million, and Honduras at 55.5 million. The bubble that began in late 2020, when imports first passed 400 million, has not burst. It has been taxed.

Here is the counterargument, and it comes from the trade's own economists. A tariff on a product with no domestic substitute is a tax on the consumer, full stop — and the United States produces almost no large cigars. One analysis put the cost of a 100 percent Nicaragua tariff at $1.26 billion in lost GDP, $2.06 billion in lost output, nearly 18,000 jobs, and $95 million in lost state and local tax revenue, with retail and wholesale trade absorbing $905 million of the output loss and more than 11,000 of the jobs. There are about 3,500 retailers and 50,000 workers whose livelihoods depend on the trade. But the counter to the counter is that the tariff was never really about cigars. It was about Nicaragua, and cigars were the leverage that happened to be lying on the table. The industry's problem is not that it was targeted. It is that it is targetable — concentrated in one country, visible, and small enough to punish without moving an election.

The deeper risk is the one the tariff fight obscures. A trade that depends on a single country's crop, a single trade agreement's rules of origin, and a single court's reading of a 1970s emergency-powers statute is a trade with three single points of failure, and 2026 exercised all three. The Supreme Court saved the industry in February. Section 122 can tax it again by spring. CAFTA-DR shields it until a future administration decides otherwise. The companies that survived the year best were not the ones with the cleverest tariff strategy. They were the ones with the deepest factory relationships, the most loyal retailers, and the customers who kept buying at $15 when the same cigar had been $12 — because the cigar, unlike the tariff, had not changed.

The implication for anyone watching this market is to price the policy, not the tobacco. A Nicaraguan cigar's cost now carries a tariff line that can move 10 points in a month, a court decision that can erase it overnight, and a trade-agreement shield that holds until it does not. The $155 Cohiba and the $75 Padrón are not anomalies. They are the top of a market that has learned to charge what scarcity and status will bear, while the middle of the market — the $8 to $15 cigars that built the boom — absorbs every policy shock first. Buy the cigar you love, from the maker who did not change the blend to save a dollar. The tariff will change again. The blend, if the maker is honest, will not.
